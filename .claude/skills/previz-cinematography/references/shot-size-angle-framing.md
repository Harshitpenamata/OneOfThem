# Shot size, angle and framing

Distilled from StudioBinder's guides on shot sizes, camera angles and camera framing. These are paraphrased notes, not quotes.

## Shot sizes (abbreviation – what's in frame – what it does)

- **Establishing** – any shot that orients us to a location at the head of a scene. It doesn't have to be wide or aerial; it can even be a POV.
- **EWS / ELS, extreme wide** – the subject is tiny against the location. Use it for isolation, scale, distance or unfamiliarity.
- **WS / LS, wide or long** – the subject is complete with plenty of environment above and below. Relationship to place; narrative distance; can dwarf a character.
- **FS, full** – head to toe fills the frame with some scenery. Good for body action and group staging.
- **MWS / MLS, medium wide (3/4)** – knees up.
- **CS, cowboy** – mid-thigh up. Registers action at hip level (hands reaching into a pocket, drawing something) plus emotion.
- **MS, medium** – waist up. The workhorse for dialogue. Keep it as the "buffer" size so a later close-up lands harder.
- **MCU, medium close-up** – chest up. Face plus small gestures and body language. Keeps a slight distance even in close conversation, which can feel cold or uneasy.
- **CU, close-up** – head to shoulders, or an object filling the frame. For emotions and reactions; save it for moments that matter to the character.
- **ECU, extreme close-up** – part of the face (eyes, lips) or a tiny detail. Shows what would otherwise go unnoticed. Often a macro lens.

Rule: shot size sets lens, camera placement, blocking and continuity. Plan the size progression across a scene. Contrast between sizes is what signals importance.

## Angles and heights

- **Eye level** – neutral, connective, "like meeting someone". Less common than you'd think.
- **Shoulder level** – the cinematic default. Slightly reduces headroom and puts the eye line just above the lens, so it reads as a hint of low angle.
- **Hip / cowboy level** – useful when one character sits or kneels and the other stands, or for action at the hip.
- **Knee level** – emphasises walking or creeping feet; paired with a low angle it adds dominance.
- **Ground level** – feet, puddles, walking without revealing the face; makes the viewer actively read the performance.
- **Low angle** – looking up. Power, menace, dread. Often paired with a high angle on the other character.
- **High angle** – looking down. Vulnerability or powerlessness (with exceptions).
- **Overhead / bird's eye (about 90° down)** – a neutral, omniscient or "divine" view; it diminishes the characters.
- **Aerial** – very high; scale and awe; establishes large spaces.
- **Dutch / tilt** – the horizon is rolled. Disorientation, unstable mind, tension. Use it as emphasis at the peak of a beat, after level shots.

Shorthand: low = empower, eye = connect, high = depower, Dutch = disorient, overhead = omniscience, aerial = awe and scale.

## Framing (who and how many)

- **Single** – one featured subject. A **dirty single** has another character's shoulder in the foreground but still features one person.
- **Two-shot / three-shot** – two or three featured subjects; lets performances play in one take and shows the relationship. A three-shot saves cutting between three singles.
- **OTS, over the shoulder** – the subject seen past the near shoulder of another character. The dialogue standard; orients the viewer and connects the characters.
- **OTH, over the hip** – like OTS, but past the hip. With uneven heights (one standing, one kneeling or sitting) it implies a power imbalance.
- **POV** – what a character (or object) sees. Sandwich it: character looks → POV → reaction.
- **Insert** – a detail that directs attention: what a character looks at, or something the characters miss (dramatic irony).

Considerations for any frame: **size** (bigger in frame = more important), **relationships** (who shares the frame and how far apart), **balance** (weight on the left vs the right).
